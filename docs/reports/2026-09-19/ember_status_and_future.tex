\begin{center}
{\Large\bfseries Ember: the long life of a $0.1\Ms$ star}\\[8pt]
{\small Working paper, 19 September 2026}
\end{center}

Ember is a one-dimensional stellar evolution code that calculates a star's
structure, composition and energy transport as it ages.
We follow a $0.1\Ms$ star with time-dependent hydrogen, helium, carbon and
nitrogen abundances, convection and microscopic diffusion, including a moving
metal group. The calculation with metal settling reaches $4.002\Tyr$,
with $T_{\rm eff}=4612$ K, central $X=3.528\times10^{-8}$,
surface $1-X=0.001805$ and surface $Z=3.154\times10^{-42}$.
Its center ceases convection near $3.551\Tyr$. The changing total metal abundance affects the material
properties, interior opacity, atmosphere and transported heat. Central hydrogen crosses $X=0.001$
near $3.650\Tyr$ and $X=0.0001$ near $3.660\Tyr$; hydrogen burning
continues outside the depleted center. The computed luminosity reaches a
local maximum of $0.006513\Ls$ near $3.673\Tyr$, at $T_{\rm eff}=4128$ K,
and then declines. Surface temperature passes a shallow local maximum of 4754 K
near $3.838\Tyr$, then declines by 141.5 K
over 164.3 Gyr. The present model remains powered mainly by
hydrogen burning, which supplies 97.28\% of its photon luminosity; a long cooling sequence is not yet established.

A continuation with the supplied white-dwarf atmosphere covers an additional
0.7546 Gyr and reaches $4.003\Tyr$, at 4632 K.
Figure~\ref{fig:wd_response} shows this atmosphere comparison, which develops
a helium-3 burning pulse and a convective shell near the hydrogen--helium
transition. Locally refined calculations follow the growth of the pulse;
its maximum and subsequent evolution remain unresolved.



A comparison calculation holds the material metal abundance fixed while
evolving hydrogen, helium and the CN isotopes. It reaches $3.704\Tyr$,
with $T_{\rm eff}=3702$ K, central $X=0.0001309$ and surface $X=0.8905$.
Central hydrogen crosses $X=10^{-3}$ near $3.661\Tyr$; burning continues
outside the depleted center. Its luminosity reaches $0.005163\Ls$ near
$3.677\Tyr$ and then decreases by 11.39\% over 27.05 Gyr to
$0.004575\Ls$. Surface temperature has a much shallower maximum near
3705 K at $3.689\Tyr$. These values describe the fixed-metal material comparison. The metal-settling calculation reaches a hotter, shallow local
temperature maximum, as described above.

The aim is to calculate the transition to a helium white dwarf, cooling
through 1000, 500 and 100 K, and subsequent evolution through disappearance
under explicit nucleon-decay scenarios. Section~\ref{sec:lifetime} gives
the lifetime timeline and its conditional alternatives.

We compare the model with Laughlin, Bodenheimer and Adams (1997; LBA97),
using their stated values and approximate curves read from their figures.
We have generated an f77-source code that seeks to replicate LBA97's exact
assumptions and methods. Figures~\ref{fig:consistent_cn} and
~\ref{fig:hydrogen_evolution} compare the evolving carbon/nitrogen calculation
directly with LBA97.

A comparison calculation omits microscopic element separation and uses
hydrogen/helium burning. It reaches $3.890\Tyr$, with
$T_{\rm eff}=5546$ K, central $X=0.0008157$ and surface $X=0.1730$.
Its nuclear and transport assumptions differ from those of the main
calculation. Figure~\ref{fig:lba_hr} shows both models.

\section{The star and its evolution so far}
The initial hydrogen mass fraction is $X_{\rm H}=0.7$, the initial $^3$He
fraction is zero, and the metal mass fraction is $Z=0.02$. The metals follow
the relative elemental abundances of Grevesse and Sauval's GS98 mixture, with
the remaining initial mass in $^4$He. The calculation holds baryonic mass fixed and omits rotation,
magnetism, mass loss and external heating. Its age excludes formation and
pre-main-sequence contraction.

The comparison sequence ending at $3.890\Tyr$ contains the initial stellar model and 9168 successive
models after accepted time steps, each resolved into 512 mass points. Each time
step evolves the abundance profile and solves the stellar structure and energy
balance, including energy released or absorbed as the structure changes.
The calculation uses 220 gas atmospheres, 72 equation-of-state composition
tables, and Los Alamos ATOMIC radiative opacities for the
high-temperature interior \cite{colgan16,tops}. The latter include additional
hydrogen-poor mixtures through $X=0$, used above $T=5.012\times10^5$ K;
the lower-temperature opacity tables retain their separate composition limits.

At their respective luminosity maxima, the metal-settling model is 26.13\%
more luminous and 10.03\% smaller in radius than the fixed-metal calculation.
The central temperatures are nearly equal, while the envelope structure and
the hydrogen-bearing layers differ substantially.

\subsection{Diffusion and the remaining hydrogen supply}
A matched diffusion comparison holds the material metal abundance fixed and
starts both calculations from the same $3.560\Tyr$
structure and evolves them for 50 Gyr, with the nuclear network and other
material physics held fixed. With microscopic drift, central hydrogen reaches
$X=0.02300$; without it, $X=0.06864$. Surface hydrogen is $X=0.2907$ and
$X=0.1847$, respectively, and effective temperatures are 3404 K and 3302 K.
Despite the much larger central depletion, total remaining hydrogen differs
by only 0.2767\%. The diffusion calculation burns 4.241\% more hydrogen during
the interval and ends with 20.10\% greater luminosity. The principal effect
over this interval is redistribution of fuel and the associated structural
response. An independent comparison with the exported diffusion routine of
Thoul, Bahcall \& Loeb \cite{thoul94} gives outward hydrogen drift at the
14 sampled interfaces with weak electron degeneracy; Ember's speeds are
0.8163--0.9258 times that classical reference. The strongly degenerate core
requires the actual gravity and appropriate electron thermodynamics;
ideal-gas pressure cannot replace the pressure supporting the star
\cite{paxton18}. This comparison supports the rate scale without establishing
the accuracy of every transport approximation.

For a nearly homogeneous radiative center, a drift speed of order
$D m_p g/(kT)$ gives a redistribution time
\begin{equation}
 t_{\rm sep}\sim\frac{kT}{4\pi G\rho m_pD}.
\end{equation}
At $T\sim9$ MK, $\rho\sim300\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and
$D\sim0.7\,\mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}}$, this is of order 100 Gyr; electric-field
and mixture factors change the coefficient. The diffusion coefficient here
comes from the adopted collision model. The estimate explains why separation
can compete with the final core-burning phase while having little effect
during the much longer fully convective phase.

LBA97 holds the envelope composition nearly fixed after the radiative core
forms and gives no microscopic element-diffusion prescription. Its final
whole-star hydrogen fraction is slightly above 0.01, although the envelope
has $X=0.155$. A useful residual-fuel scale is
\begin{equation}
 t_{\rm fuel}\sim10^{12}\,\mathrm{yr}
 \left(\frac{\Delta M_{\rm H,burn}}{10^{-3}\Ms}\right)
 \left(\frac{L}{10^{-4}\Ls}\right)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
This energy can be supplied only if the hydrogen reaches layers hot enough
to burn. Diffusion changes that supply and the envelope's thermal structure;
a central hydrogen threshold alone does not determine the subsequent
shell-burning duration or cooling age.

The fixed-metal comparison uses the same FreeEOS source through
$X=0.90$. Its late gas-atmosphere grid contains 48 source states
over $X=0.70$--0.90, $X_3=0$--0.03, 3400--4000 K and
$\log g=4.9$--5.15. In warm convective layers, a bounded continuation of $\ln\kappa$
in hydrogen abundance uses the final source interval, while intermediate
and hot layers use hydrogen-dependence ratios from the original OPLIB tables,
matched to the retained opacities at $X=0.75$. Temperature and density stay
within their source ranges. A 100 Myr comparison against retained composition
coverage changes luminosity by 0.0005780\%; this is a local sensitivity,
not an error bound for the full evolution.

Time intervals are checked by comparing one full step with two half steps,
including structure, species, total hydrogen and energy conservation.
Increasing the separate maximum abundance-change guard from 0.001 to 0.005
reduces CPU time by 24.99\% in a matched 1 Gyr calculation. Its luminosity
differs by 0.0005727\%, while the time-error criteria remain unchanged.


\subsection{Metal settling and atmospheric opacity}
The material properties depend on the changing total metal abundance,
with fixed GS98 relative proportions. The transport approximation gives the
metals a common velocity while retaining their distinct collision charges
and heat-flow variables. Selective elemental separation and changes in the
relative opacity contributions remain separate refinements. Artificially
retaining metals can keep the envelope too opaque and the photosphere too
cool. The following controls measure that sensitivity.

We calculate gas-atmosphere controls at $X=0.85$, $X_3=0$,
$T_{\rm eff}=3800$ K and $\log g=4.9$. One reduces carbon and nitrogen
to 0.1531 and 0.1541 of their initial abundances, respectively, as measured
at the surface of the $3.697\Tyr$ model. Two others reduce every metal to
0.5 or 0.17 of its initial abundance. Removed metal mass is replaced by
helium-4 at fixed hydrogen. These common reduction factors are sensitivity
choices, not predictions of individual settling rates. At
$T=3521$ K and $\rho=5.995\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, the
Rosseland absorption means relative to the unchanged mixture are 1.199
for C/N removal and 0.2344 for the 0.17 all-metal case; at 5228 K they
are 0.9869 and 0.2728. These material means exclude scattering and grains.

The coupled C/N atmosphere changes matching temperature by $+3.314\%$
and gas pressure by $-4.205\%$. Halving all metals changes them by
$-8.606\%$ and $+53.69\%$, respectively. Applying each pair of measured
boundary changes to the same $3.694\Tyr$ structure and evolving for
100 Myr gives:
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}\toprule
Atmosphere change & $\Delta T_{\rm eff}$ (K) & $\Delta L/L$ & $\Delta R/R$ \\\midrule
C/N removal & $-30.38$ & $-1.019\%$ & +1.142\% \\
All metals halved & +142.9 & +5.041\% & $-4.983\%$ \\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{center}
Both stellar comparisons pass the ordinary time and conservation checks.
The interior material tables remain fixed. The source boundary factors are
held constant over each comparison, so they are local sensitivities rather
than atmospheres recomputed at every stellar state. The 0.17 all-metal case
is an atmosphere comparison only; its matching temperature and pressure
ratios are 0.8547 and 2.813. The finite stellar tests establish an appreciable
surface-temperature response. They do not determine the actual element
distribution or a corrected temperature maximum. The latter requires metal
transport and its feedback on envelope and atmosphere opacity.

A passive transport calculation follows C, N, O, Na, Mg, Si, Ca and Fe on
the prescribed H/He structures from $3.551\Tyr$ to $3.704\Tyr$. It includes
the changing convective envelope, concentration gradients and conservative
transport between mass shells, using the published collision coefficients
\cite{thoul94}. Treating each metal as a trace species changes its drift
speed by less than 3\% relative to the full mixture at the four sampled
envelope bases. With fully stripped ions and classical heat flow, the final
surface abundances are 0.06213--0.08217 of their initial values. Suppressing
heat flow gives 0.2491--0.3076. These treatments are alternative
approximations, not uncertainty bounds. Halving the time interval changes
the surface results by at most 0.2078\%.

Figure~\ref{fig:metal-transport} illustrates the depletion and accumulation
below the envelope. The calculation holds the H/He structure fixed to the
prescribed history and omits opacity feedback, nuclear conversion of the
passive C/N tracers and changes in ionization. It therefore guides the
required material coverage rather than supplying a corrected stellar track.
The depletion nevertheless supports replacing fixed envelope metals by
transported abundances.

For the calculation with metal settling, we solve for the independent mass
fractions of hydrogen, helium-3, carbon-12, carbon-13, nitrogen-14 and inert
metals; helium-4 closes the mass sum. Nuclear captures change the actual
metal mass. All heavy elements share one mass velocity, while their charges
and heat-flow variables remain distinct in the collision calculation.
This grouping approximates differential settling among the metals.
The material free energy varies with hydrogen, helium-3 and total metal
mass, retaining the GS98 relative pattern. Its derivatives determine both
chemical forces and transported enthalpy. The energy equation includes
enthalpy carried by the total redistribution of hydrogen, helium-3 and
metals, including convection.

The variable-metal material family covers all 23 sampled structures along
the hydrogen-burning evolution. At the fully convective $3.509\Tyr$ model,
direct FreeEOS comparisons give maximum pressure and adiabatic-gradient
differences of 0.03028\% and 0.09056\%. Heat capacity and the first adiabatic
exponent differ by up to 0.7642\% and 1.037\%. These are source-interpolation
uncertainties; thermodynamic consistency and the independent stellar mass
and energy checks remain enforced.

The low-density FreeEOS domain includes the warmer atmospheric matching
states. Thirty-six independent source comparisons there agree within
0.009197\% in the material responses. Retaining the interior free-energy
values preserves every accepted structure and abundance in a matched
1 Gyr comparison.

The composition domain extends from $Z=0$ to 0.08. Twenty-eight independent
FreeEOS source comparisons, including higher-metal core states, agree within
0.02530\% in the material responses. Two composition interpolations differ
by less than 0.001 K in the surface temperature of a 10 Myr stellar comparison.

Settling raises the central metal abundance above $Z=0.03$. For larger
atomic metal fractions, through $Z=0.08$, we use a linear continuation of
the radiative opacity from the actual $Z=0.02$ and 0.03 sources:
\begin{equation}
 \kappa(T,\rho,X,Z)=\kappa(T,\rho,X,0.03)
 +\frac{Z-0.03}{0.01}
 [\kappa(T,\rho,X,0.03)-\kappa(T,\rho,X,0.02)].
\end{equation}
All contributing source temperature and density bounds remain enforced.
An alternative uses the original OPLIB GS98 mixture tables to supply the
composition ratio at fixed temperature and density \cite{colgan16,farag24}.
At 34 sampled core states, transferring this ratio from $Z=0.02$ to a
withheld $Z=0.03$ source differs by at most 0.1867\% from the ATOMIC value.
Over a matched 5 Gyr interval, the linear and ratio calculations differ by
0.001212\% in luminosity and 0.004120 K in surface temperature, with the
same convective boundary. A 400 Myr comparison near $3.688\Tyr$, where
electron conduction dominates the core, gives a luminosity difference of
$3.176\times10^{-7}\%$. The linear continuation permits evaluation at
denser core states inside the underlying source domains without extending
the OPLIB density range. These comparisons bound the measured stellar
response over the specified intervals.


As settling depletes the envelope below $Z=0.01$, the molecular opacity
interpolates the actual AESOPUS source values, including the $Z=0$ and
0.004 tables. At higher temperatures, the opacity is continued linearly in
$\kappa$ at fixed temperature, density and atomic hydrogen abundance, using
the $Z=0.01$ and 0.02 source values, with a lower limit of $Z=0.0001$.
A logarithmic continuation provides a sensitivity comparison. Over 10 Gyr,
crossing below surface $Z=0.01$, the two tracks differ by 0.002433\% in
luminosity and 0.009728 K in surface temperature, with the same convective
boundary. Independent OPLIB source comparisons at sampled envelope states
give linear-continuation discrepancies up to 1.951\% at $Z=0.006$ and
4.010\% at $Z=0.004$. In the more depleted envelope sample, the discrepancy
reaches 9.237\% at $Z=0.003$. These source differences remain uncertainty
in the opacity's composition dependence. Temperature and density stay
within the contributing source ranges.

The atmospheric metal response is measured from matched gas atmospheres at
$Z=0.01$ and $Z=0.02$. The reference atmosphere retains its helium-3
dependence; its small isotope response and the metal response are assumed
separable over $X_3\leq0.003$. An independent source at $X=0.225$,
$X_3=0.0015$, $Z=0.015$, $T_{\rm eff}=3400$ K and $\log g=5.025$
differs from interpolation by 0.6093\% in matching temperature and 2.125\%
in gas pressure. Increasing that source column's bottom depth changes
matching temperature by only 0.0002576\%. Propagating the measured paired
discrepancy into a 100 Myr radiative stellar test changes effective temperature
by 0.01507 K and luminosity by 0.001590\%. The tested envelope is close to
the reference metal abundance; this small response does not bound the error
of a strongly depleted envelope.

The measured metal response includes source pairs through 4400 K,
with the reference isotope grid's missing states kept explicit. A separate
source at $X=0.6$, $X_3=0.0015$, $Z=0.015$, $T_{\rm eff}=3600$ K and
$\log g=5.025$ differs by 0.2322\% in matching temperature and 3.064\% in
gas pressure. These sampled interpolation differences remain part of the
atmospheric uncertainty.

For $0.005\leq Z<0.01$, measured $Z=0.005$ and 0.01 atmosphere pairs
continue the boundary response. A separate source at $X=0.45$,
$X_3=0.0015$, $Z=0.0075$, $T_{\rm eff}=3700$ K and $\log g=5.1$
differs by 1.671\% in matching temperature and 1.980\% in gas pressure.
After 30 Gyr, a stellar calculation with this smooth boundary perturbation
differs by 0.3110\% in luminosity, 12.74 K in surface temperature and
0.01228\% in total remaining hydrogen, with the same convective boundary.
The largest local relative differences in trace carbon isotopes are about
3.7\%; local hydrogen, helium, nitrogen-14 and inert-metal differences
remain below 2\%. These are physical-boundary sensitivity estimates;
independent mass and energy conservation remain enforced in each calculation.

Twelve actual $Z=0.0025$ and 0.005 atmosphere pairs cover the more depleted
boundary through 4400 K. At an independent source with $X=0.65$,
$X_3=0.0015$, $Z=0.0035$, $T_{\rm eff}=4100$ K and $\log g=5.1$,
the matching temperature differs by $-0.03091\%$ and gas pressure by
$+0.2311\%$. Two 4000 K atmosphere columns ending at optical depths of
1000 and $4.075\times10^4$ differ by 0.001301\% in matching temperature
and 0.002243\% in gas pressure. The response joins continuously at
$Z=0.005$ and 0.01; source and isotope states without support remain excluded.

Eight $Z=0.001$ and 0.0025 source pairs cover the depleted
atmosphere over 4000--4400 K. At $X=0.8$, $X_3=0.0015$, $Z=0.00175$,
$T_{\rm eff}=4300$ K and $\log g=5.1$, the interpolated matching
state differs from an independent source by $-0.2539\%$ in temperature
and $+3.111\%$ in gas pressure. A stellar boundary perturbation reproducing
this measured difference changes luminosity by $-0.09315\%$, radius by
$+0.2422\%$, surface temperature by $-6.010$ K and total remaining hydrogen
by $+0.001339\%$ at $3.677\Tyr$, with the same convective boundary.
These quantify one source uncertainty; they do not bound all atmosphere errors.

Eight $Z=0.0005$ and 0.001 atmosphere pairs extend the measured response
through the next depleted-envelope interval. At $X=0.875$, $X_3=0.001$,
$Z=0.00075$, $T_{\rm eff}=4250$ K and $\log g=5.1$, the interpolated
matching state differs from an independent atmosphere by $+2.031\%$ in
temperature and $-2.755\%$ in gas pressure. A boundary perturbation that
matches this independent source vanishes above $Z=0.001$ and increases
linearly below it. Over a matched 1.250 Gyr evolution, ending at
$3.687\Tyr$ and surface $Z=0.0009185$, it changes luminosity by
$+0.1047\%$, radius by $-0.2419\%$, surface temperature by $+6.251$ K
and remaining hydrogen by $-0.0002063\%$. The convective boundary is
unchanged. Both calculations retain the same time-step, species-mass and

An additional transport check places total luminosity and the enthalpy
carried by moving species on the same mass surfaces. This matters because
integrated energy conservation can conceal a local error: in an isothermal,
constant-pressure composition-exchange test, averaging luminosities stored
at mass nodes leaves a spurious thermal flux when the composition flux is
evaluated between nodes. For an isolated active interface, the residual is
half the transported enthalpy flux. A finite-volume energy equation removes
this mismatch while preserving the species mass volumes.

respectively, with maximum temperatures of 19.95 MK. Both reach the
thermodynamic table's upper temperature; neither value is an established
flash maximum. A direct ionization calculation supports extending the
microscopic transport prescription from 2 to 1.8 MK at the base of the
nearly pure hydrogen envelope. The heat-transport transition is unchanged.
This local extension does not cover the full remnant cooling sequence.

The surface abundances describe an isolated, nonrotating model without
external accretion or magnetic fields. Accretion can maintain atmospheric
metals against settling; the result depends on the supply rate, mixed-envelope
mass and settling time \cite{bauer19}. The finite-convection pulse calculation
sets semiconvective and thermohaline mixing coefficients to zero. The reaction
$^3$He+$^3$He$\rightarrow{}^4$He+$2p$ can lower the mean molecular weight and
produce an inversion that drives thermohaline mixing, as found in red-giant
models \cite{charbonnel07}. Whether this occurs strongly enough to alter the
present shell requires an assessment of its composition gradient and transport
times, rather than assuming that the highly stratified envelope persists.

dependence of the pulse therefore remains unresolved.

A related physical example occurs in slowly accreting white dwarfs: helium-3
can accumulate through incomplete proton--proton burning and ignite a convective
layer before the principal nova outburst \cite{denissenkov13}. The fuel supply
and stellar structure differ from those of the isolated low-mass remnant, so
this precedent supports the mechanism without establishing the present pulse's
amplitude or outcome.

convergence of the complete pulse is not established by this local test.

not measured errors. They identify where the conventional composition-shifted
mixing-length formula and separately transported composition enthalpy require
a consistent treatment of parcel heat loss. The uncertain gradual-mixing
controls therefore do not establish a preferred pulse trajectory.

The burning shell is only partly degenerate. Before shell convection,
the cell contributing most nuclear power lies at $m/M=0.9612$ and 10.72 MK.
Its thermal expansion coefficient
$\delta=-(\partial\ln\rho/\partial\ln T)_P=0.9099$ is close to the ideal-gas

a thermal instability without requiring strongly degenerate shell material.
Heat transport, expansion and composition changes must be included to

Species conservation and full-step versus two-half-step comparisons are
checked throughout. During the pulse, the independent first-law residual
is measured against the larger of total nuclear power and photon luminosity,
with a relative limit of $2\times10^{-8}$; the photon-relative residual is
also recorded. This avoids demanding excessive cancellation accuracy when
nuclear heating greatly exceeds the escaping luminosity. The atmosphere
comparison remains separate from the stated main trajectory.

This comparison also uses a bounded dense-core opacity extension over
0.25--0.45 keV. Radiation carries at most $0.2535\%$ of the local
temperature-gradient transport in the affected sampled layers; conduction
dominates. Matched 1 Myr calculations with the nominal, unsuppressed and
more strongly suppressed opacity give indistinguishable surface temperatures

\subsection{Luminosity and the nuclear timescale}
An initially $0.1\Ms$ star with $X=0.7$ contains $0.07\Ms$ of hydrogen.
Taking about $6\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}}$ of deposited energy gives
a nuclear budget of order $8\times10^{50}$ erg. Its burning time scales as
this budget divided by its mean nuclear luminosity. During full convection,
this can be written $dt\simeq-M q_{\rm eff}\,dX/L_{\rm nuc}(X)$, with
$q_{\rm eff}$ accounting for neutrino losses and energy temporarily stored
in $^3$He. A persistently lower luminosity therefore lengthens the nuclear clock.

The initial Ember/LBA97 luminosity ratio is 2.006, close to the ratio 1.943
of their ages at maximum $^3$He. At their early luminosity minima, the F77
AJR and Ferguson models have $T_{\rm eff}=3218$ and 3278 K and luminosities
0.001331 and $0.001405\Ls$. Their luminosity ratios relative to LBA97 are
3.193 and 3.370; the corresponding $^3$He peak-age ratios are 3.323 and 3.632.
The peak abundances differ, so this is an approximate comparison of stages.
The ratios change later in evolution; a single time rescaling is insufficient.

A simple structural estimate gives the same scale. At fixed mass and
composition, homologous ideal-gas interiors have $\rho_c\propto R^{-3}$
and $T_c\propto R^{-1}$. Taking $\epsilon_{pp}\propto\rho T^\nu$ with
$\nu\simeq4$--6 and equating nuclear power to
$4\pi R^2\sigma T_{\rm eff}^4$ gives
\[
R\propto T_{\rm eff}^{-4/(\nu+5)},\qquad
L\propto T_{\rm eff}^{4(\nu+3)/(\nu+5)}
\simeq T_{\rm eff}^{3.1\text{--}3.3}.
\]
The Ember/LBA97 temperature ratio then predicts a luminosity ratio
1.959--2.029, including the radius adjustment. Partial degeneracy and
nonideal thermodynamics limit this estimate. The thermal times
$GM^2/(RL)$ are only about 3.0 and 5.5 billion years, respectively;
a starting thermal imbalance cannot itself sustain a trillion-year offset.
The relevant difference is the equilibrium sequence maintained by the physics.

The low LBA97 luminosity was already a recognized issue in 1997. Their
section 3.1 reports a poorer fit to the observed mass--luminosity relation
than the Burrows models, while noting uncertainty in the bolometric
corrections used for the Hyades comparison. The independent solar-metallicity
model of Chabrier and Baraffe \cite{chabrier97}, at $0.1\Ms$ and 1 billion
years, gives 2811 K, $0.1240\Rs$ and $0.0008511\Ls$, close to Ember;
its helium fraction is 0.275 rather than 0.28. Thus the cooler LBA97 model
also differed from contemporary detailed atmosphere calculations.
Chabrier and Baraffe find that a grey Eddington boundary generally makes
low-mass models hotter, so the use of a grey atmosphere alone does not
explain LBA97's lower temperature. Controlled atmosphere, opacity and EOS
substitutions are needed to identify its cause.

the approximate abundance stated by LBA97 at its central-core transition. The figure does not justify a more precise age comparison.
The luminosity--temperature track alone can conceal this difference because
it does not show how long the star spends at each position.

LBA97 used a grey atmosphere, approximate opacity changes as hydrogen is
consumed, and older interior opacities and nuclear rates. Ember uses
frequency-dependent gas atmospheres, composition-specific opacity tables,
a different equation of state and updated pp-chain rates. Grain formation
is still omitted from Ember's evolving atmosphere. These differences are
candidates for the changed surface properties and hydrogen-consumption rate;
comparisons that change one ingredient at a time are needed to separate them.
Agreement with LBA97 and computing time alone do not establish physical
accuracy.

\subsection{Core transport by radiation and conduction}
At the center of the metal-settling model at $3.688\Tyr$, electron
conduction contributes 99.79\% of the local transport driven by a temperature
gradient. The conductive and radiative coefficients are
$1.058\times10^{14}$ and $2.221\times10^{11}$
$\mathrm{erg\,cm^{-1}\,s^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, respectively; heat carried with
diffusing species is accounted for separately. The central electron
degeneracy parameter is 15.39. These local values do not imply that radiation
is unimportant in the envelope.


The pp comparison first departs from full convection near $3.546\Tyr$, at central
hydrogen fraction $X=0.1751$. By $3.560\Tyr$, the convective mass fraction
has fallen to 0.6277 and central hydrogen to $X=0.1518$, while surface
hydrogen remains at $X=0.1747$. This separation of central and surface
composition shows that convection no longer mixes the whole star.

At $3.890\Tyr$, the model has a core containing 97.43\% of the mass that
transports heat by radiation and conduction, surrounded by a convective envelope.
Central hydrogen has fallen to $X=0.0008157$, while the envelope remains
near $X=0.1730$. Radiation and conduction can carry the required energy
through this core without convection; the composition gradient also suppresses
convection. Conduction supplies 91.29\% of the local heat flux at the center,
with radiation carrying the remaining 8.712\%. Conduction is therefore already
significant in the central region. Figure~\ref{fig:transition} shows
the declining convective mass and the final abundance profile on this track.
The first loss of full convection does not by itself establish a central
radiative core. The final profile establishes the core but does not determine its formation
age. LBA97 reports central
radiative-core formation at $5.742\Tyr$.

``Fully convective'' describes where convection occurs, not the fraction of
the luminosity it carries. Radiation, conduction and convection share the heat
flow continuously. The model's mixing-length calculation makes the convective
contribution approach zero at a stability boundary. Chemical mixing uses a
stronger approximation: each connected convective region becomes uniform
rapidly. Slow composition mixing is included in some marginally stable regions,
but their additional heat transport still needs attention.

\section{Why more atmosphere calculations are needed}
The atmosphere calculations are reusable. They are performed
separately and stored as tables of boundary temperature and pressure. During
stellar evolution, Ember interpolates those tables; it does not solve
frequency-dependent radiative transfer at every time step. New atmosphere
calculations are needed when the star reaches a composition, surface
temperature or gravity not adequately covered, or when the physical treatment
changes.

The plotted track uses 220 gas atmospheres, spanning the combinations of
surface temperature, gravity and composition that it visits, with source
models extending through 5600 K.
The tabulated source states include hydrogen fractions from 0.1 to 0.7 and
$^3$He fractions from zero to 0.12. These outer ranges do not imply that every
combination has been calculated: interpolation requires a complete set of
surrounding source models, including those needed for derivatives.

Independent atmosphere calculations test the interpolation. At two 3700 K
models with compositions between the tabulated values, matching temperatures
differ by $-0.4837\%$ and $-0.7399\%$, and gas pressures by $+0.5707\%$
and $+0.5137\%$. Changing the lower boundary of the source atmospheres
changes matching values by at most $0.007883\%$ in the specified comparisons.
All 220 source models have support in the selected 72-table equation of state.
Independent comparisons with numerical-electron FreeEOS at 4736 states give
a maximum heat-capacity difference of $0.2952\%$; 2240 states also pass
thermodynamic identity checks. At the final stellar model, independent
FreeEOS evaluations at all 512 actual compositions, temperatures and densities
differ by at most 0.1012\% in heat capacity. Interpolation uses thermodynamically
consistent source states and excludes unsupported derivative stencils.
These are sampled comparisons, not error bounds across the full table.

Independent matching-state comparisons between 3900 and 5500 K differ by at
most 0.5986\%. Lower-boundary comparisons at 4000--5600 K change the matching
values by at most 0.008695\%.

For further evolution, a 300-model gas-atmosphere grid extends through
6400 K. At hydrogen fractions $X=0.15$ and 0.2, it includes
$\log_{10}[g/(\mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}})]=5.7$ and 6.0 over 5400--6400 K,
for $^3$He fractions of zero and 0.12. Six independent atmospheres between
these composition, temperature and gravity values differ from the tabulated
matching temperatures and pressures by at most 0.4860\%. Seventeen
lower-boundary comparisons change matching values by at most 0.009372\%.
All 300 atmospheres have equation-of-state support. Equilibrium chemistry
finds no condensates in the 48 structures covering the higher gravities.
These calculations extend the available warm gas boundary; the plotted
trajectory uses the 220-atmosphere grid described above.

The high-temperature ATOMIC opacity tables include additional mixtures with
$X=0$, 0.025, 0.05 and 0.075 at three metal fractions. Twelve independent
composition comparisons on hot stellar-profile states differ by at most
$0.05257\%$. This extends the composition coverage needed as central hydrogen
is depleted; the lower-temperature tables have their own composition limits.

Dense radiative transport also depends on how the plasma changes photon
propagation. An opacity extension evaluates this effect from the ATOMIC
frequency spectrum and combines radiation with conduction. On the saved
$3.890\Tyr$ structure, it changes the combined opacity by at most 3.333\%.
Independent comparisons in the hot interior agree within 0.2871\%, but
comparisons in the connection to cooler tables differ by as much as 1.194\%.
A consistent treatment across that temperature interval is required before
this extension can be used to evolve the star. Its frequency and composition
sampling must cover the denser envelopes near the beginning of the track as
well as the present structure. The
\doc{docs/PLASMA_OPACITY.md}{plasma-opacity calculations} give the physical
assumptions and numerical comparisons.

An independent opacity comparison uses the monochromatic Opacity Project
(OP) data \cite{seaton05}. We mix the element spectra using their tabulated
ion populations and include collective electron scattering \cite{boercker87}
and the OP screened free--free correction. At $X=0.1$, $Z=0.02$,
$T=9.284\times10^4\,\mathrm{K}$ and
$\rho=0.05337\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, the OP Rosseland mean is
$1.464\times10^5\,\mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$, compared with
$1.497\times10^5\,\mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}$ from TOPS on the same frequency
mesh, a difference of 2.165\%. The mixture corrections lower the OP mean
by 1.499\% at this point.

The OP table covers 25 warm-envelope layers of the computed star,
from $2.169\times10^4$ to $1.346\times10^5\,\mathrm{K}$, including all
eight layers outside the MESA OPLIB mean-table range. All required corrected
spectra are positive. The family spans 53 hydrogen fractions from $X=0$ to
0.75 at three metal fractions. Independent composition comparisons at all
18 native temperatures have a maximum difference of 0.4752\%, or 0.4192\%
at the star's mapped metal fraction. All 69 thermal, density and composition
derivative comparisons on 23 warm-envelope layers pass. Density refinement
changes their means by at most 0.001646\%.
These comparisons measure interpolation within the adopted atomic physics.
The OP mixture omits P, Cl, K and Ti, whose combined mass fraction is
$2.276\times10^{-5}$, and the dense core lies outside its grid.
The free--free correction assumes classical electrons and ionic Debye
screening. If the opacity remaining after free-electron scattering is
absorption, plasma refraction raises the warm-envelope means by
0.05659--1.623\%. Allowing that opacity to be either absorption or scattering
gives interval widths of 0.03189--0.8259\%, relative to their lower limits.
These bounds retain the supplied frequency interval and do not bound missing
elements or thermodynamic interpolation errors.

Eleven layers at $1.440\times10^5$--$2.736\times10^5\,\mathrm{K}$ lie
between the OP table and the corrected hotter opacity. The original MESA
mean tables cover all eleven. Their ordinary means differ from the TOPS
low-temperature table by 1.167--3.995\%, with TOPS lower. A consistent
spectral plasma correction and smooth source connections remain required.

We also compare the Los Alamos OPLIB mean tables distributed with MESA
\cite{farag24}. At 1940 states with $X=0$ or 0.05 and $Z=0.01$ or 0.03,
the median difference from our saved TOPS means is 2.364\%, reaching 7.279\%.
Enlarging the original table's density interpolation from four
to six points leaves a maximum difference from TOPS of 7.197\%.
The plasma-cutoff setting also fails to explain the largest discrepancy.
These sources use different element sets: MESA includes F, Sc, V and Co in
addition to our 21 elements. On the 15 warm-envelope layers supported by both
archives, OP differs from MESA by $-9.730$ to $+4.619\%$, after mapping the
isotope number densities and interpolating in composition. These differences
include atomic physics and mixture differences. A smooth connection to the
molecular and high-temperature opacities, with their uncertainties assessed,
is required before using this family for evolution. The
\doc{docs/OPACITY_ALTERNATIVES.md}{opacity comparisons} give the source
conventions, numerical checks and physical limits.

\subsection{Condensates and a hydrogen-free atmosphere}
The current evolving atmosphere uses gas-only opacity. Independent chemistry
checks found no condensates in the tested 3000--3200 K atmospheres, but found
them in the cool upper layers of 2600--2800 K models. Effective temperature
alone is therefore insufficient to decide whether grains form. Checks on the
warm hydrogen-poor structures through 6400 K find no condensates;
colder structures still require atmosphere calculations with grain formation
and opacity included.

Grain formation can affect radiation in two ways: it removes absorbing atoms
and molecules from the gas, and any suspended grains absorb and scatter light.
A calculation that isolates gas depletion assumes the condensed material
settles out. At solar composition and 2800 K, gas depletion changes the
interior matching pressure by 0.1246\% and temperature by $-0.02347\%$.
These are local gas-depletion effects, not measurements of grain opacity or
of a stellar lifetime change. Dusty atmosphere models become relevant around
2800 K and below \cite{chabrier00}. A separate grain-opacity calculation uses published optical data and explicit
grain-size choices. It passes comparisons with an independent scattering program
and analytic small-particle limits. Applied to a fixed gas atmosphere, it
combines the optical properties with the computed alumina abundance. The maximum condensed mass fraction is
$1.094\times10^{-4}$. For compact grains of radius $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$,
the integrated grain optical depths near $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ are 0.002610
for absorption and 0.002829 for scattering. These values describe one fixed
2800 K gas profile with suspended grains, not a recomputed atmosphere or a
prediction of grain size. Absorption and scattering remain separate. Coupling these opacities to the atmospheric structure requires consistent
material phases, wavelength coverage and thermal response, as described in the
\doc{docs/GRAINS.md}{grain-opacity notes}.

Grain formation also changes thermodynamics through redistribution of atoms
among molecules, ions and solids. Comparing gas-only and gas-plus-grain
chemical equilibria at one test state gives a heat-capacity correction of
$1.175\times10^5\ \mathrm{erg\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, compared with
$2.890\times10^5$ from counting grains alone. A consistent thermodynamic
reference, abundance normalization and treatment across phase boundaries are
required before this contribution can enter the evolving model.

The atmosphere wavelength-opacity tables cover material temperatures through
$1.315\times10^4$ K. Nonfinite absorption near He~I 4471 \AA\ prevents use
of the present source at $1.500\times10^4$ K. Atmosphere calculations must
remain within the valid opacity domain, with matching conditions insensitive
to the chosen lower boundary.

A hydrogen-free atmosphere presents a further issue. The source programs'
abundance normalization and molecular initialization currently depend on
hydrogen. A direct opacity test at 4017 K shows that multiplying all number
abundances by the same factor preserves composition and nearly preserves
electron density, but can change individual absorption coefficients by a
factor of 27.3. This one-temperature test does not measure a stellar-boundary
error; it shows that a simple abundance rescaling is not yet a verified solution.
The hydrogen-free limit therefore requires a source formulation that does not
normalize abundances to hydrogen.

\section{Energy and the cold helium remnant}
The discrete energy equation used for stellar evolution is
\[
 L_\gamma=L_{\rm nuc,dep}+L_{\rm grav}-L_{\nu,\rm thermal},\qquad
 L_{\rm nuc,rest}=L_{\rm nuc,dep}+L_{\nu,\rm nuclear}.
\]
Here $L_\gamma$ is the photon luminosity, $L_{\rm nuc,dep}$ is nuclear heat
left in the star, and $L_{\rm grav}$ is the contribution from changing internal
and gravitational energy. Nuclear and thermal neutrinos are counted separately.
At $3.890\Tyr$ in the 512-point model, in solar luminosity units,
\[
 L_{\rm nuc,dep}=0.01077,\quad
 L_{\rm grav}=3.201\times10^{-5},\quad
 L_{\nu,\rm nuclear}=3.362\times10^{-4},\quad
 L_{\nu,\rm plasma}=1.260\times10^{-7}.
\]
The changing-energy term refers to the last implicit half step; the other
reported diagnostics use the saved state. Before rounding, the discrete
fractional luminosity residual at this saved endpoint is below $8\times10^{-11}$. This checks the equations implemented
in the code, not an independent exact energy integral or the absence of
missing physical processes.

Cold degenerate electrons have a small thermal response superimposed on a
large temperature-independent contribution. Paired numerical integration
around the Fermi surface retains the small response without subtracting
nearly equal large terms. Pure-helium tests at 100--$10^4$ K and densities
$10^3$--$10^6\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ agree with the expected low-temperature
limit to about $10^{-10}$ fractionally at 100 K. Electron entropy and ionic
translational entropy, including mixing, are also implemented and checked
against thermodynamic identities. These analytic components have not yet been
combined into the production equation of state for a remnant.

Liquid and solid free energies and their derivatives are compared with
Ioffe EOSFI22. Across sixteen forced-phase tests, the largest checked
density-response discrepancy is $1.05\times10^{-6}$ in ideal-ion pressure
units. This tests derivative consistency within each phase; phase selection
requires comparing the free energies themselves.

Helium melting requires consistent liquid and solid free energies, including
quantum and mixture effects \cite{baiko22}. Specifying a melting value of
$\Gamma=175$ alone does not determine the latent heat or liquid heat capacity.
The connection to partially ionized outer layers remains unfinished.
Dense, cool atmospheres also require nonideal
chemistry and pressure-dependent absorption, including collision-induced
absorption; refraction and dense-fluid effects need checking \cite{blouin18}.

\subsection{Element separation in the dense interior}
Microscopic diffusion allows hydrogen, helium and metals to move relative to
one another under gravity, pressure and temperature gradients, and the
electric field that maintains charge balance. It can alter the hydrogen
supply to burning layers after convection ceases to mix the whole star.
At the center of the $3.890\Tyr$ model, the temperature is 0.1506 times
the electron Fermi temperature, so electron degeneracy must enter the
transport calculation.

A separate calculation solves the coupled species equations with zero net
baryonic mass flux and electric current. Numerical scattering integrals
supply ion collision coefficients, while electron collisions are averaged
over the finite-temperature Fermi distribution. At three layers in the
saved structure, replacing the Born approximation for electron scattering
with direct quantum scattering changes the total resistance to prescribed
hydrogen and helium motions by at most 0.1918\%. This comparison includes
all 21 distinct nuclear charges of the adopted mixture, assuming fully
stripped ions, and two specified screening choices. It measures the effect
of that scattering approximation on the selected motions; it does not give
stellar settling velocities. The forces must also agree with the equation
of state. In particular, diffusion depends on how free energy changes with
composition. Agreement in pressure and heat capacity alone does not test
these chemical forces.

The fixed-metal comparison uses an equation-of-state representation with 152 tables
at 38 hydrogen fractions and four $^3$He fractions: 0, 0.03, 0.06 and 0.12.
It interpolates the material free energy smoothly in both composition
directions, with the ideal isotope-mixing contribution evaluated analytically.
The metal inventory remains fixed. At 102 compositions spanning neutral,
partly ionized and dense material, independent FreeEOS comparisons give a
maximum difference of 0.1499\% in the matrix of second composition
derivatives. First composition derivatives agree within
$2.269\times10^{-5}\,k_{\rm B}/m_{\rm u}$ when applied to specific free energy
divided by temperature. Both source precision and composition spacing are
checked independently. Across 7936 further pressure, energy and thermal
comparisons, the largest heat-capacity difference is 0.1967\%; at the 512
points of the saved stellar structure it is 0.1062\%. All 300 warm atmosphere
nodes and 48 interpolated boundary states are supported. These sampled tests
establish the accuracy of the represented mixture derivatives; they do not
provide derivatives for arbitrary changes in the metal abundances or a
complete cold-remnant equation of state.

Composition derivatives for the species present are also evaluated when
another species is exactly absent, as $^3$He is in the initial model.
No artificial minimum abundance is introduced. The implementation passes
128 analytic comparisons, including trace-abundance limits. At zero $^3$He,
five independent FreeEOS comparisons of the hydrogen curvature agree within
0.007181\%. The chemical potential of an absent species has a singular
ideal-mixing limit; transporting that species into an initially empty cell
still requires a conservative treatment of the finite flux.

A corresponding diffusion-force calculation uses these material free-energy
derivatives and imposes zero net baryonic mass flux and electric current in
the species momentum equations. It passes 510 checks in 72 analytic mixtures,
including trace populations and nonnegative frictional heating. These tests
use synthetic collision coefficients, so they do not yet predict transport
rates in the star. The force definition follows the separation of chemical
and thermal driving described by Beznogov et al.\ \cite{beznogov16}.
When electrons are degenerate, the coefficients describing diffusion driven
by temperature gradients and its associated heat transport must use the
same definition. The usual classical heat-flow equations cannot simply be
retained without checking this consistency \cite{paxton18}.

The energy carried by each mass exchange is obtained from the same material
free energy. At six comparison states, these enthalpy differences agree with
independent FreeEOS derivatives within 0.0007647\%. The coupled classical
collision equations reproduce both the Burgers velocities and the full
material energy flux, with nonnegative entropy production.

For electron heat transport, we compare an approximation using mean drift
and one heat variable with a solution retaining the full dependence on
electron energy. Both describe elastic scattering from fixed ions in a
screened Coulomb potential, using finite-temperature Fermi statistics.
In 422 hot layers containing 99.18\% of the stellar mass, the approximation
gives 27.82--81.43\% of the conductivity at zero electron number current.
These calculations assume fully stripped ions and compare screening by
electrons alone with screening by electrons and ions. At the center, using
classical statistics instead of Fermi statistics, while retaining the full
energy dependence, gives only 33.27--38.07\% of the corresponding conductivity.
The particle density, temperature and screening length are held fixed in
that comparison. Both the energy dependence and degeneracy therefore matter
for this collision model.

Integrating out the electron energy dependence gives a small collision matrix
for the mixture. Independent integrations recover the prescribed electron
current, heat flow and dissipation, allowing these coefficients to be reused
without a discretized electron-energy solve at each stellar step.
Electron--electron collisions, higher-order ion recoil and correlations, and
relativistic effects remain outside this comparison. These results do not measure an error
in the conductivity used on the plotted stellar track.
The \doc{docs/DIFFUSION_ELECTRON_HEAT.md}{electron heat-transport calculation}
gives the equations, independent checks and remaining physical requirements.

We also evaluate electron--electron scattering in the screened Born
approximation, including exchange of the outgoing electrons and Pauli
blocking. Its ten energy modes are combined with the electron--ion collision
matrix using the actual ion abundances before reducing the mixture to its
drift and heat currents. Independent solutions retaining all modes agree
with this reduction; mass, current and entropy checks pass.
A 54-point table of the pair collision matrix covers all 422 hot layers and
both screening choices. Interpolation preserves positive dissipation and the
exact momentum null mode. Eight independent stellar comparisons, withheld
from the table, and three additional direct controls have a maximum relative
drift/heat response difference of 0.03798\%.
Across the hot nonconvective region, including electron--electron collisions
gives 0.5277--0.8780 times the conductivity of the same elastic-only model,
evaluated at zero represented element flux. Increasing from eight to ten
modes changes the interpolated stellar conductivities by at most 0.003532\%.
They do not measure an error in the conductivity selected for the plotted
track. The \doc{docs/ELECTRON_MIXTURE_COLLISIONS.md}{mixture collision calculation}
gives the normalization and remaining physical approximations.

Applying the coupled ion/electron comparison to the saved density,
temperature and composition gradients gives provisional microscopic velocities.
The calculation uses the EOS chemical derivatives and their matching material
enthalpy differences, screened ion collisions and the leading ion recoil
term, including the electron--electron matrix. The comparison covers 397 hot
nonconvective layers containing 97.43\% of the mass. The mass-weighted mean

Ember's transport equations use one shared flux at each cell face. Tests of
species and energy transport conserve both quantities and increase entropy;

Within a homogeneous convective region, local burning and abundance changes
still require internal material redistribution. Its total outward species rate
is reconstructed from the discrete continuity equation,
\begin{equation}
 F_i = F_{\rm in}+\sum_{j=b}^{i} w_j
 \left[S_j-\frac{X_j^{\rm new}-X_j^{\rm old}}{\Delta t}\right],
\end{equation}
where $w_j$ is the nodal mass and $S_j$ is the nuclear abundance source.
The rates at the region boundaries are retained; any regional imbalance
remains an explicit residual. Subtracting the microscopic rate gives the
redistribution required to maintain a homogeneous composition.

The \doc{docs/CONVECTIVE_SPECIES_REDISTRIBUTION.md}{convective redistribution analysis}

A full-star thermal comparison carries material energy according to
\begin{equation}
 Q_{\rm carried}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\left(h_{{\rm EOS},k}J_{{\rm total},k}
 +h_{{\rm kinetic},k}J_{{\rm micro},k}\right),
\end{equation}
where $k$ labels the two independent species rates,
$J_{{\rm total},k}$ includes convective redistribution,
$h_{{\rm EOS},k}$ contains the material partial-enthalpy differences,
and the kinetic term follows microscopic drift only. The EOS composition
dependence in $du/dt$ describes local storage, while these fluxes describe
spatial transport. The rates are held fixed within each structure solve and
updated in an outer iteration that also checks their heat contribution.
Independent derivatives of the native hot response agree within
$3.735\times10^{-10}$ when normalized to the derivative row.

For fluid parcels in local thermal equilibrium and pressure balance, an
optional radiation term adds
\begin{equation}
 Q_{\rm rad,comp}=\sum_k h_{{\rm rad},k}
   (J_{{\rm total},k}-J_{{\rm micro},k}),\qquad
 h_{{\rm rad},k}=\frac{4P_{\rm rad}}{\rho}
   \frac{(\partial P/\partial X_k)_{T,\rho}}
        {(\partial P/\partial\ln\rho)_{T,X}}.
\end{equation}
The coefficient is the composition derivative of radiation enthalpy per
mass at fixed temperature and total pressure. The mixing-length temperature
term already uses the total EOS heat capacity. Microscopic drift remains
separate from composition carried by fluid parcels. In the cool layers,
omitting the microscopic term also omits its radiation subtraction.
The \doc{docs/CONVECTIVE_RADIATION_ENTHALPY.md}{parcel enthalpy calculation}
gives the derivation, independent bulk-enthalpy checks and analytic derivatives.

\subsection{Carbon conversion and molecular opacity}
The small metal mass fraction does not ensure that carbon conversion has
only a small opacity effect. CO formation couples the available carbon and
oxygen; changing their relative abundances can change the molecules that
absorb radiation \cite{lederer09}. Complete conversion of the initial
GS98 carbon to nitrogen captures a hydrogen mass fraction of
$5.728\times10^{-4}$ and raises the metal mass fraction by the same amount.
An EOS approximation must account for that fuel and energy without
misidentifying the physical helium abundance. A calculation of core
exhaustion must also follow catalyst mixing during and after full convection.

We compare the GS98 material opacity with compositions in which half or
all of the initial carbon has become nitrogen. The final hydrogen abundance
is $X=0.20$, with $X_3=0$, in every case. The converted mixtures include the
captured proton mass in nitrogen; helium is adjusted to preserve the total
baryonic mass, and the other metal nuclei are unchanged. The source solves
chemical equilibrium and wavelength-dependent absorption at the same
temperature and density. At
$\rho=5.995\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, the ratios of Rosseland
absorption means to the fixed-GS98 reference are:

$T$ (K) & Half the carbon converted & All the carbon converted \\\midrule
2705 & 1.225 & 1.407 \\
3521 & 1.151 & 1.263 \\
4583 & 0.9971 & 0.9912 \\
5964 & 0.9901 & 0.9744 \\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

These means cover wavelengths from 0.09 to $30\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and exclude
scattering and grains. Across the sampled densities between $10^{-8}$ and
$10^{-4}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, half conversion raises the 2705 K mean by
18.67--30.22\%. Thus a fixed metal mixture requires a measured atmosphere
comparison before it can support evolution with changing carbon and nitrogen abundances.
These are material-state sensitivities; they do not directly predict a
change in stellar effective temperature.

A coupled gas-atmosphere comparison uses $X=0.20$, $X_3=0$,

\section{Hydrogen exhaustion and remnant cooling}
Extending the track through hydrogen exhaustion requires atmosphere and
interior inputs covering the changing composition, temperature and density,
including hydrogen-free mixtures and grain formation where relevant.

As composition becomes stratified, conservative microscopic diffusion and
settling must include the electric field that maintains charge balance and
the associated energy exchange. The importance of omitted nuclear reactions,
thermal-neutrino processes and semiconvective heat transport must be assessed
in the conditions actually reached.

Hydrogen exhaustion and cooling require distinct measurements. Central
hydrogen crosses $X=10^{-3}$, $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-5}$ near
$3.650\Tyr$, $3.660\Tyr$ and $3.767\Tyr$, respectively. At the first
crossing, the star retains about $0.0147\Ms$ of hydrogen.
We will also record when nuclear heat
falls below 10\%, 1\% and 0.1\% of photon luminosity during cooling, including
any later recovery. Remaining hydrogen mass and shell burning must be followed;
a depleted center alone does not establish that burning has ended everywhere.

A consistent liquid/solid helium equation of state, conductive
transport and a dense atmosphere must allow the star to evolve through
1000, 500 and 100 K on the descending-temperature branch.
The resulting cooling ages depend on the thermal reservoir, phase changes
and atmospheric boundary.

The present calculation assumes fixed mass and no external heating.
External heating, stellar encounters and particle decay are treated as
conditional extensions of the isolated track \cite{adams97}. The timeline
in Section~\ref{sec:lifetime} includes these alternatives, cooling past 100 K,
loss of degeneracy during mass depletion, and the end of the bound remnant.
For close encounters, energy transferred into stellar oscillations is distinct
from retained heat. Gravitational radiation can remove mode energy
\cite{rathore05}; competing physical damping mechanisms must determine the
thermalized fraction before it enters a remnant-heating history.

\section{Computing time and reproducibility}
Reading the 152 equation-of-state tables is a separate startup cost.
Buffered numeric parsing reduces a matched load from 233.5 to 64.75 s,
a factor of 3.606, preserving the tested coefficients and stellar solutions
exactly. All subsequent stellar steps share those loaded tables.

The matched full-star diffusion comparison described above takes 82.83 s
of solver time, compared with 667.4 s for the stricter control: a factor
of 8.058. There are 21 and 129 implicit solves, respectively, including the
full-step and two-half-step comparisons, and no rejected intervals.
Elapsed times including one EOS table load are 127.1 and 718.2 s.
The largest accepted interval increases from 0.2460 to 2.782 Myr.
Both calculations have 512 mass points and the same physical inputs.

The abundance tolerance for the outer composition/structure iteration is
$10^{-10}$, with $10^{-11}$ inside the species solve; independent energy
and species-conservation tests remain satisfied. The local time-error
criteria are distinct from these nonlinear-solver tolerances. A safeguarded
Newton inversion of electron density replaces repeated bisection in the
collision calculation, using the same quadrature and table domain.
Among 903 scalar and 903 derivative queries, scalar responses agree within
$1.761\times10^{-14}$ and physically scaled derivatives within
$1.552\times10^{-14}$. The scalar calculation is 2.064 times faster and
the derivative calculation 1.356 times faster in alternating measurements.

A separate late 2 Gyr comparison increases the structure time-error
allowance from 0.0003 to 0.001 with the physical tables and conservation
checks unchanged. Solver CPU falls by $65.47\%$, while endpoint
luminosity changes by $1.078\%$, radius by $0.4062\%$ and temperature
by 3.114 K. These changes exceed the stated comparison limits, so the
plotted track retains the original setting.

The short comparison does not determine
the runtime of the remaining hydrogen burning or remnant cooling.
The \doc{docs/PRODUCTION_ACCURACY.md}{accuracy and timing report} supplies
the full comparison and physical scope.

The initial diffusion coefficients give an explicit stability limit of
672.7--936.1 years. Among the last 500 saved structures, steps lasting at least
one million years have a median duration of 59.44 million years. This
comparison motivates implicit transport; convergence does not determine
the time accuracy of a stellar step.

Computing the model sequences shown in Figure~\ref{fig:consistent_cn}
used 47.49 CPU-hours: 36.31 hours for the metal-settling trajectory,
10.98 hours for the fixed-metal comparison, and 2.259 hours for the pp
comparison. The first two share 861 states and 2.052 CPU-hours, counted

2.6 times larger in CPU-hours. These sequence totals include native
table loading, time-step comparisons and adaptive retries within the runs
supplying the plotted curves; independent controls and discarded tracks are
excluded. Their rough $3\times10^{14}$ FLOP estimate assumes
$2\times10^9$ operations per CPU-second, with an estimated range
$0.6$--$6\times10^9$. This is a broad conversion informed by CPU arithmetic
benchmarks, rather than a measured whole-program operation rate.
Both costs use the same unit: one CPU-hour is one processor core working
for one hour. Sequence costs are measured process CPU time. Table costs
are estimated from serial job durations and, for some EOS batches, elapsed
time multiplied by the number of workers. Documented sleep is removed;
remaining waiting and unused batch capacity may overestimate CPU use.

Preparing physical tables is separate from integrating a stellar track.

comparison over the saved 512-point profile gives 0.3561 microseconds per
evaluation with smooth composition interpolation, compared with 0.1469
microseconds using linear interpolation of the same tables. The test includes
temperature and density responses, with repeated access to cached data;
it does not measure a stellar time step or the full diffusion calculation.
A standalone probe uses at most 4.149 GB of memory.
Table preparation and loading are reusable costs, distinct from evaluation
at each stellar mass point.

derivatives with the additional electron reuse. Both forms of reuse are included in Ember.

are extracted from accepted models and checked against the recorded output
hashes. Full raw outputs, generated physical tables, saved stellar states and
executable archives remain local. Instructions for recreating the figures
and recovering the recorded stellar calculation accompany the
\doc{docs/reports/2026-09-12/README.md}{paper files}.

The
repository supplies figure data, source specifications and validation records;
regenerating the bulk physical tables is required for stellar calculations
from a fresh checkout.
